### R&W · Command of Evidence (Quantitative) · hard · #23 (q00353)

> An agricultural economist investigated whether irrigation water use efficiency — defined as crop yield per cubic meter of water applied — differs systematically across three irrigation technologies: flood, sprinkler, and drip. He hypothesized that drip irrigation would show not only the highest average efficiency but also the least variability across crop types, making it the most reliably efficient system. He collected yield and water use data across four staple crops using each technology and computed efficiency values for each crop-technology pairing.
> Table: Irrigation Water Use Efficiency (kg of crop per m³ of water)
> | Crop | Flood | Sprinkler | Drip |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Wheat | 0.6 | 1.1 | 1.8 |
> | Maize | 0.5 | 1.0 | 2.1 |
> | Cotton | 0.4 | 0.8 | 1.5 |
> | Soybean | 0.7 | 1.3 | 1.9 |

**Q:** Which choice best uses data from the table to support the economist's claim?

- A) Drip irrigation produced the highest efficiency value for every crop in the table, with values ranging from 1.5 kg/m³ for cotton to 2.1 kg/m³ for maize, a range of only 0.6 kg/m³, while flood irrigation ranged from 0.4 to 0.7 kg/m³ — a narrower absolute range but across substantially lower values.
- B) Drip irrigation achieved the highest efficiency for all four crops, with values ranging from 1.5 to 2.1 kg/m³, a spread of 0.6 kg/m³, compared to sprinkler irrigation's range of 0.5 kg/m³ (0.8 to 1.3), demonstrating that drip is both the most efficient and — given its higher absolute values — the most reliably efficient system across crop types.
- C) Flood irrigation was the least efficient technology for every crop, confirming that modernizing away from flood systems would improve agricultural water use regardless of the crop grown.
- D) Sprinkler irrigation represented a consistent improvement over flood irrigation across all four crops, with efficiency gains ranging from 0.1 to 0.6 kg/m³, suggesting that intermediate technology upgrades can substantially improve water use efficiency.

**Key:** B · **Reviewer:** A (low)
- Also defensible: **B** — B reports the same accurate figures (drip 1.5–2.1, spread 0.6; sprinkler 0.8–1.3, spread 0.5) and makes essentially the same relative-variability argument as A, so a reader could judge it an equally good evidence statement; its only weakness is that it asserts the data 'demonstrate' least variability when the absolute spread is actually largest for drip.
- multiple_correct_answers: Choices A and B are near-duplicates: both correctly state that drip is highest for all four crops, both cite a 0.6 kg/m³ drip spread, and both appeal to drip's higher absolute values to rescue the 'least variability' part of the claim. The only distinguishing feature is B's overstated 'demonstrating' verb, which is too fine a hair for a fair item.
- unclear_or_ambiguous: The economist's hypothesis (least variability) is actually contradicted by the table in absolute terms (drip range 0.6 > sprinkler 0.5 > flood 0.3); it holds only on a relative/coefficient-of-variation basis, which no choice states explicitly. So no option cleanly 'supports' the full claim.

### R&W · Command of Evidence (Quantitative) · hard · #1 (q00331)

> The following text is adapted from a research report on avian foraging ecology.
> Biologists investigating the competitive exclusion principle frequently study the hunting strategies of predatory birds in overlapping territories. A long-standing hypothesis posits that when two closely related raptor species—the Northern Harrier and the Red-tailed Hawk—share a habitat, they divide resources via temporal segregation, meaning one species hunts primarily during early dawn hours while the other concentrates its activity during peak afternoon heat, thereby minimizing direct physical encounters. Ecologist Elena Vance designed a study to evaluate this hypothesis by recording active hunts across multiple field sites.
> Table: Average Number of Active Hunts Observed per Hour
> | Raptor Species    | Early Dawn (5 AM - 7 AM) | Midday (11 AM - 1 PM) | Late Afternoon (4 PM - 6 PM) |
> |-------------------|---------------------------|------------------------|------------------------------|
> | Northern Harrier  | 14.2                      | 2.1                    | 15.8                         |
> | Red-tailed Hawk   | 3.5                       | 18.4                   | 4.2                          |

**Q:** Which choice best uses data from the table to complete the evaluation of the temporal segregation hypothesis? The data in the table ________ the temporal segregation hypothesis because the Northern Harrier's active hunting peaked during early dawn (14.2) and late afternoon (15.8) while dropping to a minimum at midday (2.1), which perfectly alternated with the Red-tailed Hawk's hunting profile, which ________

- A) support; reached its maximum intensity during midday at 18.4 hunts per hour, precisely when the Harrier's activity was lowest.
- B) weaken; remained low during early dawn (3.5) and late afternoon (4.2), indicating that the two species consistently avoided hunting at the same times.
- C) refute; peaked at 18.4 hunts per hour during midday, demonstrating that both raptor species are highly active during the exact same intervals of the day.
- D) support; exhibited a completely uniform distribution of 18.4 hunts per hour across all measured periods of the daytime tracking cycle.

**Key:** A · **Reviewer:** A (high)
- difficulty_mismatch: Labeled 'hard,' but the distractors are internally self-contradictory (B pairs 'weaken' with evidence of avoidance; C pairs 'refute' with mismatched reasoning; D misstates the table outright), making the correct answer easy to identify.
